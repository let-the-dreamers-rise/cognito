## What does your project do?

Millions of Indian parents live alone while their children work a thousand
kilometres away. The child carries a low-grade daily fear with nowhere to put
it, and the only outlet is a phone call that is friction for both sides,
because "haan beta, sab theek hai" tells you nothing at all.

Sab Theek answers one question, once a day, in one sentence: did today go
ordinarily? "Amma's Friday looked normal. Up around 7:10, a trip to the shop,
phone on charge by evening."

It is not a monitoring product, it is a relief product. On 364 days out of 365
nothing happens, so the job is to make "nothing happened" legible. A dashboard
hands the anxiety back and asks you to interpret it; a sentence resolves it.
There is not a single chart in the app.

It is deliberately not location sharing. Location answers "where is she"; the
real question is "is she okay". A map pin is least useful exactly when risk is
highest, because she is at home either way and the dot looks identical whether
she is making tea or lying on the bathroom floor. Routine is evidence of
wellbeing. Position is not.

When a day is not ordinary it asks her first, twice, quietly, and most mornings
that ends it and her family never learns there was a question. Only silence
escalates. Past a threshold the politeness stops: two days without a human
touching the phone skips the gentle rungs and reaches the family and a
registered neighbour at once, because the family is a thousand kilometres away
and the neighbour is forty feet away and can knock on the door.

Built for adult children of parents living alone. The buyer is the anxious
child; the phone it runs on belongs to the parent. Designing for that split is
the whole product: it is why she is always asked before anyone is told, and why
the app can never be hidden on her phone.

## How did you use AWS in your project?

The architectural point first, because it is why this is event-driven on AWS
rather than a CRUD app with a chatbot attached: **the hardest event in this
system is the one that never arrives.** You cannot detect absence with an event
handler, because nothing fires. You need a clock that runs when an expected
signal did not come.

**Build it - AWS open source stack**

- **AWS CDK (TypeScript)** defines the entire stack: 59 resources in one
  deployable unit, same language as the application code.
- **AWS SDK for JavaScript v3** throughout the Lambda handlers - modular
  clients for DynamoDB Document, Step Functions, SNS and Bedrock Runtime.
- **AWS Lambda Powertools patterns** for structured JSON logging on the sweep
  and summarise jobs, so a silent failure is greppable in CloudWatch.

**Ship it - AWS services**

- **AWS Lambda** - 8 functions, Node 22 on ARM64: enrol, ingest, pulse, sweep,
  escalate, summarise, resolve, demo.
- **Amazon EventBridge (Rule)** - the absence sweep every 10 minutes. This is
  the clock the entire system turns on.
- **Amazon EventBridge Scheduler** - the nightly sentence at 21:00 in the
  member's own timezone, using native Asia/Kolkata support rather than UTC
  arithmetic.
- **AWS Step Functions** - the escalation ladder. Wait states between rungs, a
  Choice at the top that routes critical cases straight past the polite rungs,
  a Choice after the family rung that skips to the neighbour when zero pushes
  were actually delivered, and a catch on every task so a failed rung closes
  the incident instead of stranding it open forever.
- **Amazon DynamoDB** - single-table design with one GSI. Raw signals carry a
  TTL so the behavioural trail expires while the daily verdict survives; the
  privacy claim is enforced by infrastructure rather than by a promise.
- **Amazon API Gateway (HTTP API)** - 8 routes for enrolment, ingest, pulse,
  settings, stand-down and the demo controls.
- **Amazon Bedrock (Amazon Nova Lite, Converse API)** - writes the daily
  sentence. Honest note below.
- **Amazon SNS** - SMS to the registered neighbour, who will not have the app.
- **AWS Amplify Hosting** - the deployed Expo web build.
- **Amazon CloudWatch** - 8 log groups with 30-day retention, set explicitly
  because logs that never expire would contradict the product's own claim that
  the signal trail expires.
- **AWS IAM** - least-privilege per-function roles generated by CDK grants.

**On Bedrock, plainly.** The Converse call is implemented, granted and invoked
live rather than replaced with fixture text. It produces nothing on this
account: `Model invocation max tokens per day for Amazon Nova Lite` reads `0`
and is marked not adjustable, so every call returns `ThrottlingException: Too
many tokens per day`, in every region I tried. Rather than hide that, the API
returns `writtenBy: "bedrock" | "fallback"` so anyone can see which wrote the
sentence, and a deterministic writer produces every sentence in the demo. On an
account with a token allowance the same code path runs unchanged.

## Learning and growth

First time with Step Functions, and it is here because of a real lesson rather
than a checkbox. I started by trying to detect "she hasn't got up yet" with an
event handler, and it cannot be done - absence is not an event. Once that
clicked, EventBridge arming a Step Functions ladder stopped being a service I
was supposed to use and became the only correct shape for the problem.

The harder lesson was about my own code. Late in the build I audited what was
already deployed and found bugs that were all silent by construction, because
in a system whose job is to notice nothing happening, a broken detector and a
quiet day look identical:

- A DynamoDB Query used `ScanIndexForward: false` with a limit, so "the first
  signal of the day" was the earliest of the newest 200 - and the nightly job
  learns her routine from exactly that value.
- Step counts arrive as a running daily total resent every five minutes, and I
  was summing them. A walk to the shop reported six figures.
- Device timestamps were never clamped to now, so one phone with a fast clock
  would push the waking clock into the future and silence that person's sweep
  permanently.
- An empty-string timestamp slipped past a `??` fallback, was read as
  `Infinity` hours since her last movement, and assessed the quietest member in
  the system as 48 hours silent - the loudest possible alarm for no reason.
- Nothing in the app ever requested READ_SMS, so the strongest waking signal -
  a UPI payment at 8am, meaning she got up, got dressed and walked out -
  returned an empty list on every phone, forever.
- Nothing registered a background task, so signals only flowed while the app
  was open. A woman who never opened it looked exactly like a woman in trouble.

Every one of those would have shipped looking fine. That is now the thing I
design against, and it is why the escalation ladder reads delivery receipts
from Expo instead of trusting a 200, and why the API reports which engine wrote
each sentence.

## Feedback: what could be better

**Amazon Bedrock - the quota failure is undiscoverable until it bites.** My
account's `Model invocation max tokens per day for Amazon Nova Lite` is `0`
and `Adjustable: false`. Nothing surfaces that before you call the API. The
error you get is `ThrottlingException: Too many tokens per day, please wait
before trying again` - which reads like ordinary rate limiting you can retry
past, so the SDK dutifully retried three times and I spent real hours assuming
it was transient. It is not throttling, it is a hard zero allowance, and
"please wait" is actively misleading because waiting never helps. Two fixes
would have saved all of it: make the exception name the quota it hit and link
to how to raise it, and show remaining daily tokens on the Model access page
next to the model you just enabled.

**Amazon Bedrock - inference profile prefixes are not discoverable.**
`amazon.nova-lite-v1:0` and `us.amazon.nova-lite-v1:0` are both valid strings
in different contexts, and getting it wrong returns `ValidationException: The
provided model identifier is invalid` with no hint that the `us.` cross-region
prefix is required in that region, or that `ap-south-1` needs `apac.`. The
Model access console lists model names, not the identifier string you must
actually pass. Printing the exact invocable ID per region on that page would
remove an entire category of guesswork.

**AWS Amplify Hosting - a manual deployment can succeed while serving 404s.**
Amplify accepts a zip whose entries use backslash separators, reports the job
as SUCCEED, and then returns 404 for every asset because the paths do not match
what the HTML requests. PowerShell's `Compress-Archive` produces exactly that
zip on Windows. Nothing in the console or the job log indicates a problem - the
deployment is green and the site is broken. Validating entry paths at upload
and failing the job would turn a silent, hard-to-diagnose outage into an
immediate error message.

**AWS CDK with Step Functions - no state-machine-level error handling.** To
guarantee that a failed task closes my incident rather than stranding it open,
I had to call `.addCatch()` on every single `LambdaInvoke` individually. Miss
one and that failure mode silently returns. A state-machine-wide default catch,
or a construct-level helper, would make the safe thing the easy thing.

**AWS Lambda log groups in CDK.** Functions create `/aws/lambda/<name>` log
groups with never-expire retention unless you construct a `LogGroup` yourself
and pass it in. For a product that argues its behavioural trail should expire,
silently accumulating member IDs in CloudWatch forever is the opposite of the
default I wanted. Switching also left the original auto-created log groups
behind with no indication they were now orphaned.

**Amazon DynamoDB in CDK** deprecated `pointInTimeRecovery` in favour of
`pointInTimeRecoverySpecification`, but plenty of current documentation and
examples still show the old property, so you get a deprecation warning while
following the docs.

## What did you like?

**AWS Step Functions is the reason this product's core idea is expressible at
all.** The escalation ladder is genuinely hard logic - ask her, wait, check,
ask again, wait, check, tell the family, check whether anyone actually received
it, then text a neighbour - and none of that is code I had to write. Wait
states, a Choice at the top routing critical cases past the polite rungs, and a
second Choice that skips to the neighbour when zero pushes were delivered. The
single best thing: the wait duration is an input to the execution rather than
baked into the machine, so my demo waits six seconds per rung and production
waits twenty minutes, on exactly the same state machine, with no branch in my
code. The visual execution history is also how I proved the behaviour was real
- I could see, rung by rung, that a two-day silence skipped the gentle steps.

**Amazon EventBridge Scheduler's native timezone support.** Being able to say
"21:00 in Asia/Kolkata" and have it honour that, rather than doing UTC offset
arithmetic that breaks on the person who moves, removed a whole class of bug
from a product that is fundamentally about what time someone wakes up.

**AWS CDK grants.** `table.grantReadWriteData(fn)` generating a correct,
narrow IAM policy means least privilege is the path of least effort rather
than something you retrofit after a review. Ten roles across eight functions
and I never hand-wrote a policy document except for the two cases that
genuinely needed one.

**Amazon DynamoDB TTL.** My strongest privacy claim - the raw behavioural trail
expires while the daily verdict survives - is enforced by a table setting, not
by a cleanup job I could forget to write or a promise in a privacy policy. Item
expiry as infrastructure rather than application code is the right primitive
for that, and single-table design kept member, signal, incident and watcher
records in one query path.

**CloudWatch Logs Insights** made the silent-bug audit possible. Being able to
filter across a log group by pattern and see the actual Bedrock exception body,
rather than a status code, is what turned "the AI isn't working" into "the
quota is zero and not adjustable" in about two minutes.
